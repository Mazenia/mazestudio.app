You highlight your notes in three colors and reread your summary the night before. Those are the study techniques students use most, and in the research they land among the ones that pay off least.

In 2013, John Dunlosky, Katherine Rawson, Elizabeth Marsh, Mitchell Nathan and Daniel Willingham reviewed hundreds of studies on ten common techniques and published the results in *Psychological Science in the Public Interest*. They gave each technique a utility rating: high, moderate or low. They included several techniques because students report relying on them, and those came out worst.

## The two techniques with the top rating

### Testing yourself

You close the notebook and try to answer from memory, with flashcards or a past exam. This is **active recall**, also called retrieval practice. The effort of digging for the answer is what makes it stick, even when you get it wrong and then check the right one.

A 2006 experiment measured it with short passages: a week later, people who had tested themselves remembered 61% and people who had reread remembered 40%. There's more detail in [how to study for an exam](/blog/how-to-study-for-an-exam/).

### Spreading study out over time

You study the same topic in several sessions on separate days instead of all at once. If you also leave more time between one review and the next, you get **spaced repetition**. In [spaced repetition: what it is and how to use it](/blog/spaced-repetition/) you'll find the forgetting curve and a review schedule.

The two work well together: you test yourself, and you do it on separate days.

## The ones rated moderate

They work, but the authors found fewer studies on them or more mixed results.

### Asking yourself why

As you read, you ask yourself why each fact is true. "Why does the left ventricle have a thicker wall?" makes you connect the fact to what you already know: it pumps blood to the whole body. Researchers call this **elaborative interrogation**.

### Explaining it to yourself

After reading a step, you explain in your own words how it connects to the one before. It's close to what the internet calls the **Feynman technique**, after the physicist Richard Feynman: explain a topic as if you were telling someone who knows nothing about it. If you get stuck at some point, that's the part you didn't understand.

### Mixing topics when you practice

If you're practicing problems, alternate different types instead of doing twenty of the same kind in a row. On the exam they come mixed, and you have to spot which method each one needs. The review calls this **interleaved practice**.

## The ones that pay off little

Rereading and highlighting got a low rating, as did summarizing and two techniques for memorizing with images and keywords. You can use them, but don't count them as studying.

Highlighting helps on a first read, to mark what you'll turn into questions. Rereading gives you the feeling that you know it because the text looks familiar, and on the exam you have to produce it without the text in front of you. A summary helps when you write it without looking at the book, and at that point you're already testing yourself.

## The pomodoro

With the pomodoro technique you study in 25-minute blocks with short breaks. It isn't in the review, because it helps with attention more than with memory. It pairs well with the techniques above: you study during the block and test yourself during the break. Here's [the pomodoro technique with a bell instead of a clock](/blog/pomodoro-technique-bell/), and if getting started is the hard part, [how to focus while studying](/blog/how-to-focus-while-studying/).

## A week with these techniques

On Monday you read the new topic and write questions instead of highlighting. On Tuesday you answer those questions without looking and, where you miss, ask yourself why. On Thursday you answer them again, mixed with the ones from the previous topic. On Sunday you do a quick pass through everything.

It's the same hours you'd spend rereading, and you get to the exam having pulled each answer from memory several times.
