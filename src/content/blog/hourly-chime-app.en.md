For centuries, most people didn't own a clock. They had the church bell. It rang the hours across the whole town, and you knew it was noon without looking at anything. Grandfather clocks and old wristwatches with an hourly beep kept the same idea indoors.

Then phones arrived and the chime went quiet. The time is always one glance away, so in theory we don't need it. In practice, looking at your phone to check the time is how many of us lose twenty minutes to something else.

## Looking versus hearing

Checking the time is an action. You have to remember to do it, stop what you are doing and look. When you are absorbed in a task, that is exactly the moment you forget.

With a chime the time comes to you, so it still works when you are deep in something. And because you don't pick up the phone, there is nothing else on the screen to pull you in.

## Every hour is often too long

An hourly chime is great for knowing roughly where you are in the day. But a lot can slip in an hour. If you meant to spend 20 minutes on a task, the hourly bell tells you too late.

[Shorter intervals](/blog/interval-timer-android/) turn the chime into a quick check-in:

- **Every 15 minutes** for tasks that tend to swallow you, like scrolling, email or tidying.
- **Every 30 minutes** for focused work, so you notice when it is time to stand up or switch.
- **Every hour** for a calm sense of the day passing, the way the church bell did it.

## Make the sound fit the place

A chime should be noticeable [without being an alarm](/blog/reminders-that-dont-get-ignored/). In a quiet room a short, soft sound is enough. In a busy house you may want something deeper that you can hear from another room. If you share a space, vibration alone can do the job.
