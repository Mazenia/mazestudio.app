In 1994, psychologists Roger Buehler, Dale Griffin and Michael Ross asked students to predict when they would finish their senior thesis. On average they guessed about 34 days. It took them about 56. Researchers call this the **planning fallacy**: when we estimate our own tasks, we picture the plan going well and forget everything that usually gets in the way.

You can't fully switch that bias off. But you can catch it sooner, and small blocks of time are one of the simplest ways.

## Why 15 minutes

Fifteen minutes is long enough to get something real done: answer a few emails, fold a load of laundry, write a paragraph. It is short enough that losing one doesn't ruin the day. And four of them make an hour, so the arithmetic stays easy.

## How to use the rule

1. **Estimate in blocks.** Instead of "I'll do the report this afternoon", write "report: 6 blocks". It forces a concrete guess.
2. **Mark each block as it passes.** A tick on paper, a dot in a notebook, anything. You see progress, and you see when a task has eaten twice what you gave it.
3. **Decide at the edge of each block.** Keep going, switch, or take a break. Small decisions often beat one big plan made at 9 a.m.
4. **Leave empty blocks.** Two or three free blocks a day absorb the things you didn't plan for. Without them, one surprise pushes everything back.

## The hard part is noticing the edges

A plan in blocks only works if you notice when a block ends. Looking at the clock every few minutes is tiring, and you will forget the moment you get absorbed.

## Start smaller than you think

Try it for one morning, not a whole week. Plan three hours in twelve blocks, set a 15-minute chime, and write down what happened in each block. At lunch, compare. The difference between your plan and your blocks is the most useful planning information you will get all week, and it is yours, not an average from a study.
