You've got the exam date and a stack of notes. Most people read them over a few times with a highlighter in hand. It feels productive, and the day after the exam you realize you remembered less than you thought.

This plan uses the two [study techniques](/blog/study-techniques/) that come out best in research: quizzing yourself and spreading your reviews over several days. You can start it today, whether you have a month or a week.

## Start with the date and the syllabus

Count the days left and list the topics the exam covers. Ask what the exam will look like, because a multiple-choice test asks you to recognize the answer and an essay exam asks you to write it with no help. If you can get past exams, save them for the end.

Spread the new material out until a week before the exam. That last week is for review, with no new topics.

## Turn each topic into questions

As you read, write questions with their answers: "What does insulin do?", "What year did the French Revolution start?". One idea per question. If a topic gives you ten questions, that topic already has its review material.

Paper or an app both work. You need a way to cover the answer.

## Quiz yourself instead of rereading

In 2006, Henry Roediger and Jeffrey Karpicke had students at Washington University in St. Louis learn short passages. One group read the passage four times. Another read it once and then tried to write down everything they remembered, three times in a row.

Five minutes later, the rereading group came out ahead. A week later, the group that tested itself remembered 61% of the passage and the rereading group 40%. The rereaders had gone over the text about 14 times, against 3.4 for the other group, and they felt more confident they'd remember it.

Rereading fools you because it works if the exam is in five minutes. For next week, cover the answer and pull it from memory, even if you get it wrong.

## Spread your studying over several days

An hour a day for four days leaves you remembering more than four hours the night before. Review each topic the next day, after three days and after a week, and if you miss a question, see it again soon. In [spaced repetition](/blog/spaced-repetition/) you'll find the research and a review schedule.

## Mix topics when you practice

If the exam has problems to solve, don't practice twenty of the same kind in a row. In 2007, Doug Rohrer and Kelli Taylor had two groups of students practice math problems. One group worked the problems grouped by type and the other worked them mixed. A week later, the mixed group did far better.

On the exam the problems come mixed, and part of the challenge is spotting which method each one needs. If you practice that way, you train for it.

## Do a practice run with past exams

A week out, sit a past exam with the real time limit and no notes. Grade it like a strict teacher and turn each mistake into a new question. The practice run shows you which topics are shaky and whether you have enough time.

## The night before the exam

Do a quick pass through all your questions and go to sleep. If you study until four in the morning, you get to the exam sleepy, with less attention for what you do know. Pack what you need to bring, so the morning starts calm.

## If you have a week or a day left

**With a week**, pick the topics that weigh most on the exam and start your questions there. Review each topic on three different days and use the last day for a practice run.

**With a day**, forget about covering everything. Work through a past exam or the questions at the end of each chapter, go over what you missed and quiz yourself on it again in the afternoon. At night, sleep anyway.
