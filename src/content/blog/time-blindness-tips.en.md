You sit down to answer one email. You look up and the afternoon is gone. Nothing dramatic happened. You just didn't feel the time go by.

People call this **time blindness**. The term comes up a lot in conversations about ADHD. Psychologist Russell Barkley, who has written about ADHD for decades, describes it as a kind of nearsightedness to the future: what is happening now feels sharp, and everything later feels blurry. You don't need a diagnosis to recognize the feeling, though. Long screens, deep focus and boring tasks blur time for almost everyone.

What helps most is getting time out of your head and into the room, where you can see it or hear it. Here are seven ways to do that.

## 1. Put a clock where your eyes already are

A clock on the wall behind you does nothing. Put one in front of you, at the edge of your screen or on the desk. An analog clock helps more than digits, because you see how much of the hour is left as a slice of a circle.

## 2. Use a timer you can see shrink

Visual timers show the remaining time as a colored disc that gets smaller. You don't have to read a number to know you are halfway through. They work well for chores, homework and anything with a clear end.

## 3. Let a sound mark the time

Seeing time needs you to look. Hearing it doesn't. A soft sound every 15 or 30 minutes tells you that another block has gone by, even when your eyes are glued to something else. That is the whole idea behind Tolli: a little bell that dings every few minutes, so each ding is a small check-in. "Fifteen minutes since the last one. Am I still doing what I meant to do?"

## 4. Guess first, then check

Before a task, write down how long you think it will take. Afterwards, write down how long it took. After a week you have your own correction factor. Most people find they underestimate, and seeing your real numbers is more convincing than any advice.

## 5. Plan in blocks of time

A to-do list hides time. A day split into blocks of 15 or 30 minutes shows it. When a task has to fit in a block, you can see when it overflows.

## 6. Give transitions their own time

Getting ready, switching tasks and finding your keys all take time that rarely appears on a plan. Add a short buffer before anything with a fixed start, like a call or leaving the house.

## 7. Look back once a week

On Friday, look at what you planned and what actually happened. It shows you how your time behaves, so next week's plan is closer to real life.

## Start with one

You don't need all seven. Pick the one that matches the moment you lose time most often. If it is during deep focus at the computer, a sound works better than anything you have to look at.
