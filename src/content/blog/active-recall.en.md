You read the chapter, underline it and read it again. Everything looks familiar. Then a friend asks you one question about it and nothing comes.

Recognizing something on the page and pulling it out of memory are two different skills, and the exam asks for the second one. **Active recall** trains it: you close your notes and try to answer from memory before you check.

## What active recall is

Active recall, also called retrieval practice, is any way of studying where you produce the answer yourself. Flashcards, a past exam, a blank page where you write down everything you remember about a topic, a friend asking you questions. Rereading, highlighting and watching the lecture again are passive, because the answer is already in front of you.

The effort of searching for the answer is the part that works. Each time you retrieve something, it gets easier to retrieve the next time.

## What the research says

In 2006, Henry Roediger and Jeffrey Karpicke gave students short science texts to learn, in a study published in *Psychological Science*. Some students read the text four times. Others read it once and then wrote down what they remembered three times, without looking. Five minutes later, the rereaders did better: 83% against 71%. A week later the result flipped. The students who had tested themselves remembered 61% and the rereaders 40%.

The rereaders also predicted they would remember more. Rereading feels like it's working because the text becomes familiar, and familiar is easy to mistake for learned.

In 2011, Karpicke and Janell Blunt compared retrieval practice with concept mapping in a study in *Science*. A week later, the students who had practiced recalling did better on the final test, including the questions that asked them to draw inferences instead of repeating facts.

In 2013, a review led by John Dunlosky rated ten common study techniques. Practice testing was one of the two that got the top rating, next to spreading study out over time. Both are explained in [study techniques that work](/blog/study-techniques/).

## How to use active recall to study

### The blank page

After a class or a chapter, take a sheet of paper and write down everything you remember, without looking. Then open your notes and mark what was missing in another color. Those gaps are what you review next.

### Questions instead of headings

Turn each heading in your notes into a question. "The Krebs cycle" becomes "What goes into the Krebs cycle and what comes out?" Cover the answer and try first.

### Flashcards with one idea each

One question per card, and a specific one. If a card needs a whole paragraph to answer, split it into two or three cards.

### Explain it out loud

Explain the topic as if to someone who missed the class. The point where you get stuck is the point where you need to go back to your notes.

### Past exams, without notes

If you can get past exams, do them in exam conditions and grade yourself afterwards. If the date is already set, [how to study for an exam](/blog/how-to-study-for-an-exam/) has a plan from today until that day.

## Getting it wrong also counts

Active recall feels harder than rereading, and you'll miss answers. That's fine as long as you check the right answer straight away. In 2009, Nate Kornell, Matthew Hays and Robert Bjork found that trying to answer a question before learning it, and failing, still helped people remember the answer later, as long as they saw it right after.

Active recall works best spread over several days: you try today, again in a couple of days and again next week. That's [spaced repetition](/blog/spaced-repetition/), and it's also how you slow down the [forgetting curve](/blog/forgetting-curve/).
