You study a topic on Monday and by Thursday you can barely explain it. Your memory isn't failing you. That's the normal speed of forgetting, and a German psychologist measured it more than 140 years ago.

## Where the forgetting curve comes from

In the 1880s, Hermann Ebbinghaus used himself as the only subject. He memorized lists of nonsense syllables, three-letter combinations like WID or ZOF, so that nothing he already knew could help him. Then he waited a set amount of time, from 20 minutes to a month, and relearned each list.

He measured how much less time the second round took. If he had needed 10 minutes the first time and only 6 the second, 40% of the work was still saved somewhere in his memory. He published the results in 1885, in a book called *Memory*.

## What the curve shows

These are the savings Ebbinghaus reported:

| Time after learning | Work saved when relearning |
|---|---|
| 20 minutes | about 58% |
| 1 hour | about 44% |
| 9 hours | about 36% |
| 1 day | about 34% |
| 6 days | about 25% |
| 1 month | about 21% |

Most of the loss happens in the first hour. After the first day the curve flattens out, and what's left fades slowly.

## What the 2015 replication found

In 2015, Jaap Murre and Joeri Dros repeated the experiment and published it in *PLOS ONE*. One of them spent about 70 hours learning and relearning syllable lists, following Ebbinghaus's method. The curve came out very close to the original.

They also found a small bump at 24 hours. Savings were a little higher than the curve predicted, and the authors suggested sleep as a possible reason.

## What the curve doesn't say

- **Your notes aren't nonsense syllables.** Ebbinghaus chose material with no meaning on purpose. Things that connect to what you already know are forgotten more slowly.
- **It measures relearning, not answering.** The figures are about time saved on a second round. Figures you see online like "you forget 70% in a day" usually mix this up.
- **It's one person.** The shape has held up for a long time. The exact percentages change with the person and the material.

## How to flatten the forgetting curve

Ebbinghaus also noticed that each time he relearned a list, the next round of forgetting was slower. That's the useful part for an exam.

### Review before it's gone

A first review the next day, then after three days, after a week and after two weeks. Each review lands as the memory starts to fade. That's [spaced repetition](/blog/spaced-repetition/).

### Quiz yourself instead of rereading

Pulling the answer out of memory leaves a stronger trace than reading it again. That's [active recall](/blog/active-recall/), and it pairs well with spaced reviews.

### Connect it to something you know

Ask yourself why each fact is true, or find an example from your own life. Material with meaning falls off the curve more slowly than a list you memorize blind.

### Sleep on it

Research on sleep and memory points the same way as the bump at 24 hours: a night's sleep after studying helps keep what you learned. An all-nighter before the exam skips that step.
