It's the night before the exam and you're on your third pass through your highlighted notes. Everything looks familiar. The next morning you read the first question and the answer won't come.

Rereading gives you the feeling that you know it. In the exam you have to pull it out of memory, and you train that by quizzing yourself. **Spaced repetition** is one of the best-studied ways to do it: you review each topic on separate days and leave more time between one review and the next.

## What spaced repetition is

You review a topic today, again in a couple of days, then after a week and then after a month. Each review lands as the memory starts to fade, and each time you pull it back it lasts longer.

The idea is more than a century old. In 1885, the German psychologist Hermann Ebbinghaus memorized lists of nonsense syllables and measured how much he forgot as the days went by. From that data he drew the **forgetting curve**: you lose most of what you learn in the first days, and after that the loss slows down. He also saw that each review left the curve flatter.

## What the research says

In 2006, Nicholas Cepeda and his team pulled together 317 experiments from 184 articles in a review published in *Psychological Bulletin*. They found that splitting study across several sessions leaves you remembering more than studying it all in one go, and that the best gap between reviews grows the longer you need to remember.

In 2013, John Dunlosky and four other psychologists rated ten common study techniques in *Psychological Science in the Public Interest*. Two got the top rating: practice testing and spreading study out over time. Rereading and highlighting landed among the low-utility ones.

Spaced repetition combines both. You quiz yourself, which is what people call **active recall**, and you do it on separate days.

## How to use spaced repetition to study

### Turn your notes into questions

Write one idea per card, with the question on one side and the answer on the other. "What does helicase do?" works better than "DNA replication" because it makes you answer something specific.

### Answer before you look

Say the answer out loud or in your head, then check the card. The effort of digging for the answer is the part that trains your memory.

### Be honest when you grade

If you missed it, the card comes back soon. If it came without effort, it can wait weeks. Marking a half-guess as correct takes away the review you needed most.

### Let the gaps grow

One possible schedule for a new topic: the next day, after three days, after a week and after two weeks. If you miss a review, you go back to the short gap.

On paper, the **Leitner system** does this with boxes. Each card you get right moves to a box you review less often, and each one you miss goes back to the first box.

### Start weeks ahead

If the exam is a month away, start today with a few cards a day. Cramming everything into the last week leaves no room for the gaps to grow.
